A backtesting laboratory built to measure how much its own results should be believed.

Summary#

Any strategy that looks good in a backtest looks good for one of two reasons: it found something real, or it was the luckiest of the many variants that were tried. The backtest itself cannot tell you which. That ambiguity is not a detail — it is the central problem of quantitative finance, and most tooling ignores it entirely.

AlphaGlyph is a backtesting engine with the second question built in. It simulates strategies on real historical prices, then subjects every result to the statistical tests that quantify selection bias: the Probabilistic and Deflated Sharpe Ratios, Combinatorially-Symmetric Cross-Validation for the Probability of Backtest Overfitting, Fama-French factor decomposition, and a forward-only prediction ledger that cannot be retroactively edited.

Pointed at its own strategy grid, the system reports a 72% probability of backtest overfitting (SPY, run 2026-08-01) — the in-sample winner lands in the bottom half out-of-sample more often than not. That is the headline result, and it is a negative one.

  • 19 REST endpoints, 298 automated tests in CI
  • Indicators implemented from first principles (SMA, EMA, RSI, MACD, ADX, Bollinger)
  • Live at alphaglyph.org

1. The problem: a backtest is a contaminated experiment#

A backtest looks like an experiment, but it usually violates the rule that makes experiments meaningful: the hypothesis is chosen after seeing the data.

The mechanism is simple. Suppose you test 42 variants of a moving-average strategy and keep the one with the best Sharpe ratio. Even if none of the 42 has any genuine edge, the maximum of 42 noisy estimates is systematically above zero. You will always find a winner. Its in-sample Sharpe is not an estimate of its true performance — it is an estimate of true performance plus a selection premium, and nothing in the backtest separates the two.

The standard response is to report the winner's Sharpe ratio and move on. AlphaGlyph treats that as the bug, and the rest of this report describes the instrumentation built to measure it.

2. The demonstration: the Data-Mining Lab#

Before arguing that selection bias is severe, the system demonstrates it on strategies that are known to have no edge.

backend/datamining.py generates N random long/flat timing strategies on a real price series. Each goes long on a random ~50% of days. By construction, none of them contains any information about future prices — every one is a coin flip.

The lab then backtests all N, keeps the single best Sharpe ratio, and reports two verdicts side by side:

Verdict What it assumes Typical result
Naive PSR the winner was the only strategy tried often "significant"
Deflated Sharpe N strategies were tried; corrects for it "not significant"

The gap between those two numbers is the entire lesson. The winning strategy is 100% luck — this is guaranteed by how it was generated — yet naive statistics frequently call it significant. The expected maximum Sharpe of N noise strategies is drawn on the histogram, and the winner sits exactly where chance predicts.

This is the argument the rest of the system is built to act on: a Sharpe ratio is meaningless without knowing how many were tried to find it.

3. Probabilistic and Deflated Sharpe Ratios#

Implemented in backend/stats.py, following López de Prado (2014).

3.1 PSR#

The naive Sharpe ratio assumes returns are normal and the sample is large. Financial returns are neither — they are skewed and fat-tailed, which makes the Sharpe estimate noisier than it appears. PSR returns the probability that the true Sharpe exceeds a benchmark, correcting for both:

PSR(SR*) = Φ[ (SR̂ − SR*) · √(T−1) / √(1 − γ₃·SR̂ + (γ₄−1)/4 · SR̂²) ]

where γ₃ is skewness and γ₄ raw kurtosis. Since SciPy reports excess kurtosis, the implementation uses (excess + 2)/4, which is the same quantity. All Sharpe values are held in daily units internally and converted only at the boundary — a detail that is easy to get wrong and silently changes the answer.

3.2 DSR#

The Deflated Sharpe Ratio is PSR with the benchmark raised to the Sharpe you would expect from the luckiest of N independent trials:

SR*_annual = E[max Z_k] · √(252 / T)

E[max Z_k] uses the standard Euler-Mascheroni approximation to the expected maximum of N unit normals.

The √(252/T) scaling matters and is frequently omitted in third-party implementations. The expected maximum is expressed in units of the sampling distribution of the Sharpe estimate, whose width depends on how many observations you have. Without rescaling for T, the deflation is applied at the wrong magnitude and the test is miscalibrated. With n_strategies = 1, DSR reduces exactly to PSR against a zero benchmark, which is the correct degenerate case and is asserted in the tests.

4. CSCV and the Probability of Backtest Overfitting#

backend/cpcv.py. Method from Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2017), via Combinatorially-Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV). An earlier version of this report called it CPCV, which is a different method: López de Prado's Combinatorial Purged CV builds many backtest paths, while CSCV measures how often the in-sample winner fails out of sample. The module and the /api/research/cpcv route keep the old name so existing links work.

4.1 The strategy grid#

The grid is deliberately made of strategies people actually tune, not strawmen:

  • Trend-following — long when fast SMA > slow SMA, else flat. Fast ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40}, slow ∈ {50, 80, 100, 150, 200}, keeping only fast < slow → 30 variants.
  • Mean-reversion — stateful RSI entry/exit: enter below a buy threshold, exit above 55. Period ∈ {7, 14, 21}, threshold ∈ {25, 30, 35, 40} → 12 variants.

42 variants total. Positions are shifted one day, so a decision made on the close of day t is held over t → t+1. There is no look-ahead. The first 205 rows are dropped to clear the longest indicator's warm-up.

4.2 The procedure#

  1. Build a T×N matrix of per-period returns, one column per variant.
  2. Split the T rows into S contiguous groups (S even, clamped to 6–12; C(12,6) = 924 combinations is the computational ceiling).
  3. For every way of choosing S/2 groups as in-sample:
    • pick the variant with the best in-sample Sharpe, n*
    • find n*'s rank among all variants out-of-sample
    • ω = rank / (N+1); logit λ = ln(ω / (1−ω))
  4. PBO = the fraction of splits where λ ≤ 0 — i.e. the in-sample champion lands in the bottom half out-of-sample.

A PBO near 0.5 means selection told you nothing at all.

4.3 Purging and embargo#

Financial returns are serially correlated, so an in-sample block sitting immediately adjacent to an out-of-sample block can leak its edge straight across the seam. The implementation trims embargo rows from each end of every in-sample block — and critically, only from the in-sample side. Trimming both would discard out-of-sample data that is legitimately available and bias the result optimistically.

4.4 Degenerate columns#

A variant that stays flat across an entire block has zero variance and therefore an undefined Sharpe. These are assigned -inf so they can never be selected as the in-sample champion, and are filtered out before the degradation regression, medians and scatter. Silently letting a no-trade outcome count as a performance figure would distort every downstream statistic — this is the kind of edge case that turns a correct method into a wrong number.

4.5 What it also reports#

  • Performance degradation — OLS of OOS Sharpe on IS Sharpe. A slope well below 1 means the in-sample edge mostly evaporates.
  • Probability of OOS loss — how often the selected strategy actually loses money.
  • Logit histogram, IS-vs-OOS scatter, and a split diagram for the interface.

4.6 Calibrated by simulation#

Checking a formula on one worked example shows the code matches the paper. It does not show the statistic does its job. backend/tests/test_stats_calibration.py checks the property each statistic exists for, on simulated data where the right answer is known (seeded, two years of daily returns):

Check Should be Measured
PSR at 95% calls pure noise significant (normal returns, 2,000 draws) about 5% 5.05%
Same, fat-tailed returns (Student-t, 4 df) about 5% 5.1%
Best of 20 noise strategies: PSR calls the winner significant often (selection bias) 63%
Best of 20 noise strategies: DSR calls the winner significant under 5% 0.5%
PBO when all 30 strategies are noise about 0.5 0.49
PBO when one of 30 has a real edge (Sharpe ≈ 2.4) near 0 0.07

The middle rows are the reason the Deflated Sharpe Ratio exists: picking the best of 20 coin flips and testing it as if it were the only one fools the plain test most of the time. DSR is conservative here, because it tests against the expected best of N. To make the simulation possible, PBO's core was split from the price-driven grid into pbo_from_matrix, which accepts any matrix of strategy returns.

5. Factor decomposition#

A strategy can post real returns and still have no alpha, if it is merely capturing well-known risk premia that a passive factor ETF sells for a few basis points. fama_french_decomposition regresses daily excess returns on the three Fama-French factors:

R_p − R_f = α + β_mkt(R_m − R_f) + β_smb·SMB + β_hml·HML + ε

and reports Jensen's alpha annualised, factor loadings, R², and per-coefficient t-statistics. Factors are the real daily series from Ken French's data library, not a proxy.

An engineering note worth recording: this test kept intermittently reporting "n/a" in production. Two causes, both environmental rather than statistical — Ken French had moved the file from /data_library/ to /ftp/, and Dartmouth blocks the default python-requests user agent from some cloud IPs. The fix was a browser-like UA, both URLs tried in order, retries with backoff, and a disk cache of the last good copy as an offline fallback. A statistical test is only as reliable as its data pipeline, and this one was failing for reasons that had nothing to do with statistics.

6. Calibration and the forward ledger#

Two components address a claim the backtest cannot make: whether the forecaster's probabilities are honest.

calibration.py — the model emits statements like "~58% chance of an up move." A forecaster is well-calibrated when, across every day it said 60%, the market actually rose about 60% of the time. Predictions are binned into deciles to produce a reliability diagram and scored with Brier score and log loss, each against a "always predict the base rate" baseline so the number means something.

ledger.py — the harder test. Every directional call is written to an append-only table at the moment it is made, with price, p_up, direction and horizon. Once the horizon elapses, the row is graded against what actually happened. Nothing is ever edited or deleted. The resulting hit rate and Brier score are a genuine forward record that cannot be retro-fitted.

Calibration measures the model on historical out-of-sample data already in hand; the ledger measures it on the future, one real day at a time. Grading is lazy — a plain GET matures any elapsed rows, so the record stays current without a cron job.

The live ledger turned out not to accrue. After three months it held three graded calls. The backend runs on Render's free tier with no database configured, so the table lived in a SQLite file on an ephemeral disk that every redeploy wiped, and it only recorded predictions a visitor happened to ask for. A forward record that resets is not a forward record.

So the forward record now lives in git (tools/forward_ledger.py, run by .github/workflows/forward-ledger.yml). Every trading day near the close, the live Predictor is asked about a fixed basket of ten tickers (SPY, QQQ and eight large caps), and each call is appended to frontend/data/forward_ledger.csv and committed. Calls whose horizon has passed are graded against the first close on or after the due date and filled in once. The commit history is the audit trail: each call sits in a commit dated before its outcome existed. The first scheduled runs started about three hours late, and a logging window that closed at 16:15 ET would have recorded nothing. Calls are now accepted from 15:15 ET until midnight New York time, priced at that day's close, which is still days before the outcome. A third, later schedule is a backup. Both records grade with the same rules (track_record.py), and /health now reports which database the live ledger is using.

The window-dependence is itself a result#

An earlier run of the identical procedure reported PBO 63% with ~38% Sharpe retention. This run reports 72% and ~52%. Same code, same grid, same ticker — a different rolling window.

That instability is not a defect in the measurement; it is the measurement working. A single PBO number is a statement about one window, and anyone quoting it as a fixed property of a strategy has made the same category error the metric exists to catch. This report therefore dates every figure.

The degradation slope is negative#

The regression of out-of-sample Sharpe on in-sample Sharpe has a slope of −0.59. A slope near 1 would mean in-sample performance predicts out-of-sample performance; a slope near 0 would mean it predicts nothing.

A negative slope means the relationship is inverted — across these splits, the variants that looked best in-sample tended to do worse out-of-sample. Selecting on in-sample Sharpe was actively worse than selecting at random.

What the 5% OOS-loss rate means#

Worth stating precisely, because it is easy to over-claim. The selected strategy loses money out-of-sample only about 5% of the time. The failure is not that these strategies are ruinous — it is that choosing among them on in-sample performance does not identify the good ones. The money is mostly fine; the selection procedure is what's worthless.

The one clean positive, with its conditions: a dip-buying strategy on AAPL/MSFT from 2021-01-01 to 2024-01-01 returned +21.6% in ranging markets against SPY's +5.6% over the same days — an excess of +16.1 percentage points. Both conditions are load-bearing. The regime one is the obvious one; the window is the other: extending the run to 2025-01-01 shrinks the ranging-market excess to +3.6 points, because 2024's ranging days rewarded simply holding the index. It is not a claim that the strategy works in general. (Rerun on 2026-10-27; the July figures, +21.8% vs +5.6%, differed only by data revisions.)

The AUC of 0.51 is worth stating plainly. The machine-learning forecaster, which took the most work to build, cannot predict next-week direction. It is reported at 0.51 rather than quietly dropped, because a system whose entire premise is honest measurement does not get to hide its own null results.

A modality ablation: do macro and news inputs help?#

The forecaster is a transformer over 60-day windows with three input blocks: price features, macro series from FRED, and GDELT news tone. The shipped checkpoint had been trained with the macro and news blocks zero-filled, so the multi-modal design had never been tested. The ablation (ml/ablation.py, output in ml/ablation.json) retrains the same architecture, with the same splits and early stopping, on three input sets: price only, price + macro, and all three. Each is trained from three seeds, and the seeds' probabilities are averaged. An L2 logistic regression on the last day's 13 features is the baseline a deep model has to beat.

Training data runs to 2020-12-23 and validation to 2023-11-29, with a 10-day purge at each boundary. Every figure below is on the test split after that: 85 tickers, 5-day direction, 60,945 windows, 54.2% of them up. Nothing was tuned on it. Uncertainty comes from a moving-block bootstrap over test dates (20-trading-day blocks, 1,000 resamples), not over rows. The 85 tickers on one day share a market and the 5-day labels overlap, so resampling rows would make every interval far too narrow. Arms are compared on the same resampled dates. News tone is lagged one day, because GDELT buckets by UTC day and a same-day value would leak. It was found for 57 of the 85 tickers, which is 67% of test windows.

Three findings, all null:

  1. Neither extra input helps. Every paired difference is under 0.01 AUC, and every interval spans zero.
  2. The transformer does not beat a linear model. The logistic regression has the highest test AUC (0.513), though not by a margin the intervals can tell apart.
  3. Validation chose the worst arm. Price + macro had the highest validation AUC and the lowest test AUC. With differences this small, picking on validation is close to picking at random, which is the selection problem of §4 in miniature.

The rule for this experiment was set before it ran: report the result whatever it is, and replace the shipped model only if validation picked a different input set and its test interval was clear of the current model's. Neither held, so the shipped model, the price-only network, is unchanged.

8. Limitations#

  • PBO is window-dependent. A single PBO figure describes one data window and one grid. It is not a universal constant.
  • The grid is only two strategy families. Trend and mean-reversion are representative of what retail practitioners tune, but a wider grid could produce a different number.
  • CSCV assumes reasonably comparable variants. With a grid mixing wildly different exposure profiles, rank-based comparison is less meaningful.
  • Transaction costs are modelled, not measured. Real fills, slippage and market impact are not simulated.
  • Option prices in the options backtester are modelled. Black-Scholes with a fitted volatility level, the real VIX term structure and a modelled skew, no dividends and no early assignment. Checked against Cboe's PutWrite index, the model tracks it but understates the worst months (§9.1).
  • The forward record is young. It accrues ten calls a trading day from November 2026. Until it holds a few hundred graded calls, its hit rate and Brier skill are noise, and a single market regime dominates it.

9. Options strategy backtests#

The options backtester extends the same question, whether an edge is real, to option strategies. It holds one position at a time on one underlying: it opens the legs, marks them to market at every close, closes on the first of expiry, a days-before-expiry rule, a profit target or a stop loss, and opens the next position the same day. Units are sized so that each position's worst case at expiry (or, for uncovered short calls, the loss on a 30% rally) uses at most a set share of the account. Idle cash earns the 3-month T-bill.

Prices are modelled, and this is the central limitation. No free source has historical option quotes, so each leg is priced with Black-Scholes on that day's close. Three choices keep the prices close to real chains:

  1. Level. For SPY, at-the-money implied volatility is 0.835 × the VIX. The VIX is a variance-swap rate that includes the skew, so 30-day at-the-money IV sits below it; the factor is fitted to real option returns (§9.1). Other tickers use their own 21-day realized volatility times the market's implied-to-realized ratio that day (clipped to 0.8-2.0), so their IV rises in fearful markets, as real IV does.
  2. Skew. IV changes with standardized moneyness, by 0.40 per standard deviation on the put side and 0.15 on the call side: a 16-delta put trades near 1.4 × at-the-money IV and a 16-delta call near 0.85 ×. An early version used the raw VIX with a mild symmetric skew and flattered short-premium trades badly: at the same sizing, an SPY iron condor showed a 26% CAGR over 2021-2025. With the VIX adjustment and the asymmetric skew it fell to about 12%.
  3. Term structure. IV at other maturities comes from the real VIX curve that day: the VIX9D, VIX, VIX3M and VIX6M give 9, 30, 93 and 183 days, interpolated linearly in total variance, and the same ratios are applied to every ticker. Before 2011, when the VIX9D starts, the short end falls back to a modelled slope, (T / 30 days)^β with β falling from about +0.10 in calm markets to below zero in panics. That slope was the first version of the term structure. Against the real curve it was right on average at 93 days (a mean ratio of 1.113 against 1.111) but too steep at 9 days (0.888 against 0.946), and it tracked the day-to-day shape loosely (correlations of 0.46 to 0.77). Without any term structure, a calendar spread bought a 60-day option at the 30-day volatility and showed a 26% CAGR; with the real curve, it loses 1.6% a year.

Each of those corrections moved results toward what is known about these strategies, but it is still a model. Calendars and far out-of-the-money wings depend most on the modelled shapes, so they are the least reliable results. Every result in the UI carries a "model-priced" banner and the volatility source.

9.1 Checked against real option returns#

The model can be tested after all, because Cboe publishes the returns of real option strategies as indices. The S&P 500 PutWrite index (PUT) sells a one-month at-the-money put every month and holds the strike in Treasury bills; Yahoo carries its daily closes from 1996. The replica runs the same strategy in the backtester on SPY: a 30-day at-the-money put, held to expiry, fully collateralised, without costs (the index has none).

The model has one free setting, the IV level as a share of the VIX. It was fitted on 2007-2015 alone, choosing the value on a 0.75-0.95 grid whose replica matched the index's annual return (5.99% against 5.91%), which gave 0.835. Everything below is 2016 on, which the fit never saw (tools/options_validation.py, output in frontend/data/options_validation.json):

Volatility (monthly, annualised) 8.6% 9.4%
Worst drawdown −29.2% −30.0%

The previous model, 0.87 × the VIX with the modelled term structure, earned 9.7% a year on the same window, 1.5 points ahead of the index; the fitted level brings the gap to 0.5 points. The replica tracks the index's direction (correlation 0.87, tracking error 4.6% a year) and its worst drawdown, but not its worst months. Real option prices jump further in a crash than a smooth volatility model allows, so the model understates tail losses for short-volatility strategies, and its results for them should be read as an upper bound.

The replica and the index also differ by design. The index trades SPX options at Cboe's special opening quotation on the third Friday; the replica trades SPY options at the close every four to five weeks. The fitted level absorbs the average effect of those differences, so it is an effective level, not a measurement of implied volatility. The check covers one strategy, at-the-money puts; Cboe's call-writing and other indices are not on free feeds, so out-of-the-money strikes and calls rest on the modelled skew.

9.3 Where the P&L comes from#

A return figure doesn't say what earned it. Each day a position is open, the backtester splits its change in value with the previous close's Greeks: delta × the price move, ½ gamma × the move squared, theta × the days elapsed, and, for each leg separately, vega × the change in that leg's own implied volatility (legs move by different amounts because of the skew and the term structure). What the Taylor expansion misses is reported as "other": higher-order terms, rate moves, and above all the last days before expiry, when the Greeks change fastest. By construction the parts add to each trade's P&L, and with commissions, spreads and interest on idle cash they add to the account's (both are tested).

9.4 Is an options edge real?#

The options lab is a new way to fool oneself, and the tools of §3 and §4 apply to it directly. They run on the options backtester's daily excess returns.

The leaderboard is 19 tries. "Compare all strategies" runs every preset and ranks them, which is a selection from 19. On the SPY window, the luckiest of 19 strategies with no edge would show a Sharpe near 0.84 by chance. The top row, a short strangle with a Sharpe of 1.02, has a PSR of 98% on its own, which looks significant, but a Deflated Sharpe of 64% once judged as the best of 19. The cash-secured put falls from 96% to 48%. No row clears 95%, and the leaderboard now shows the deflated figure next to each Sharpe.

Settings are tries too. Options traders tune days to expiry and exit rules. The robustness test reruns one strategy at 7, 14, 30, 45 and 60 days, each held to expiry, closed at 50% profit, closed halfway to expiry, or stopped at a 100% loss: 20 backtests. It deflates the best setting's Sharpe for 20 tries and runs the CSCV procedure of §4 on the 20 return streams (10 groups, 252 splits). On SPY, 2021-11 to 2026-11:

Strategy Best of 20 settings Its Sharpe Deflated Sharpe PBO Best setting loses out of sample
Cash-secured put 7 days, stop at a 100% loss 1.90 97% 2% 0% of splits
Covered call 60 days, close halfway 0.58 27% 41% 12%
Iron condor 45 days, hold to expiry 0.49 22% 38% 59%
Long straddle 7 days, take 50% profit 0.51 22% 32% 50%

For three of the four, picking a setting is close to a coin flip: the best-looking setting lands in the bottom half of the other half of history a third or more of the time, and the iron condor's best setting loses money out of sample in most splits. These PBO figures move with the window as §7 found for stocks: ending the same test two days later, on 2026-11-08, puts the iron condor's PBO at 53% rather than 38%. The weekly cash-secured put with a stop is the exception on both tests. That result is also where the price model is weakest. Weekly options and a stop that is checked once a day at the close are the conditions under which §9.1 found the model understates sudden losses. The tests check the selection, not the prices, so the page says a pass is a reason to test with real quotes, not proof of an edge.

Two engineering notes. A sweep is 20 full backtests in one web request, so the delta-strike search, which priced all 201 candidate strikes for every new position, now bisects (|delta| is monotone in the strike) and then compares the neighbouring strikes; results are identical to the full search (tested on 300 random cases), and runs are about 2.5 times faster. Even so, a five-year sweep takes about a minute on the free hosting tier, so sweeps are limited to five years of history to stay inside the server's request time.

9.5 Entry rules, and how much of a result is the model#

Entry rules. A position can be made to open only when the VIX is in a range, when the underlying's IV rank (today's implied volatility against its past year) is in a range, or when the underlying is above or below its 200-day average. The rules gate entries only; a position that is open when a rule stops passing runs to its own exit. On the SPY window, no rule improved the plain cash-secured put:

Cash-secured put, only when… CAGR Sharpe Max drawdown Days allowed
(no rule) +9.5% 0.82 −8.2% 100%
the VIX is 20 or higher +8.2% 0.65 −8.0% 33%
the VIX is below 20 +5.4% 0.21 −10.5% 67%
IV rank is 50 or higher +6.1% 0.30 −11.7% 49%
SPY is above its 200-day average +5.9% 0.27 −11.8% 78%
SPY is below its 200-day average +6.0% 0.39 −6.6% 22%

Selling puts only when the VIX was high kept most of the return in a third of the days, the one rule that came close. Each rule is also one more try in the sense of §9.4.

Model sensitivity. Every backtest is rerun three more times with the volatility model changed: implied volatility 10% lower throughout, 10% higher, and without the skew.

Strategy As run IV 10% lower IV 10% higher No skew
Cash-secured put +9.5% +6.4% +11.1% +4.9%
Covered call +9.5% +6.2% +11.0% +10.8%
Iron condor +3.4% −1.7% +11.0% +12.5%
Long call +5.0% +16.7% −4.8% +4.8%
Long straddle −6.8% +4.6% −15.3% −6.9%

A 10% error in the volatility level is worth more than most of the differences between strategies: it turns the iron condor from a loss into an 11% a year winner and the long call the other way. Short-premium returns are, roughly, the gap between implied and realized volatility, and a 10% shift in implied volatility is a large share of that gap. This is why §9.1 matters: the PutWrite check pins the level well inside 10%, since a 0.5-point annual gap on the cash-secured put corresponds to an error of about 2% in volatility. The skew line is a reminder that out-of-the-money strikes rest on a modelled shape that the check does not cover. Each result shows the range next to its headline, flagged when it spans five points or more.

10. Reproducing#

git clone https://github.com/Danny-397/Alphaglyph
cd Alphaglyph/backend
pip install -r requirements.txt
pytest                     # 298 tests
python app.py              # http://localhost:5000

Architecture: the browser holds all UI state and the backend is a stateless compute layer; the ML model is trained offline and shipped as a portable ONNX artifact. Data resolves through a three-stage cascade — Tiingo, then yfinance, then Stooq — so a single upstream outage does not take the system down.
